Planet

MARS

Hailed as "The Red Planet" because its dusty surface regolith is heavily saturated with iron oxide (rust) minerals, casting a striking crimson-amber glow across the night sky. A dynamic, oxidized world of towering shield volcanoes, gargantuan canyon fault systems, seasonal polar dry ice caps, and two orbiting captured asteroid moons. Coated in iron oxide dust, it hosts Olympus Mons—the tallest volcano in the Solar System at 21.9 km—and Valles Marineris, a canyon system 4,000 km long spanning across the continent. Ancient river deltas and dry lake basins prove liquid water once flowed freely across its surface before the solar wind stripped its primordial atmosphere.

INNER MARTIAN MOON // FEAR

PHOBOS

Phobos is the larger and innermost of the two Martian satellites, orbiting just 6,000 km above the red sands of Mars. It speeds along faster than Mars rotates, rising in the west and setting in the east twice each Martian sol. Marked by the colossal Stickney impact crater and parallel stress grooves, Phobos is a low-density porous rubble pile.

Tidal deceleration is inexorably dragging Phobos inward at about 1.8 meters per century. In 30 to 50 million years, tidal gravitational stresses will either shatter Phobos into a shimmering planetary ring around Mars or send it crashing into the Martian crust.

✦ 01. Iron Oxide Regolith & Olympus Mons

The distinctive crimson tint of Mars stems from pervasive fine iron(III) oxide (rust) dust coating its surface rocks and whipped into atmospheric suspension by planetary dust devils. Because Mars has no open liquid oceans, its total land area (144.8 Million km²) is almost identical to the total dry continental surface of Earth.

Mars hosts the largest volcano in the Solar System: Olympus Mons, a colossal basaltic shield volcano towering +21.9 Kilometers (72,000 ft) high—nearly three times the height of Mount Everest—spanning 600 kilometers across, large enough to cover the entire state of Arizona or Poland.

✦ 02. Valles Marineris: The Grand Rift

Stretching across the Martian equator lies Valles Marineris, a gigantic tectonic chasm spanning over 4,000 Kilometers long, up to 200 km wide, and plunging over 7 Kilometers deep.

Formed as a massive tectonic crack when the neighboring volcanic Tharsis plateau swelled upward billions of years ago, Valles Marineris is four times deeper and ten times longer than Earth's Grand Canyon, spanning a distance equivalent to the entire width of the continental United States.

✦ 03. Polar Caps & Subsurface Glaciers

Mars possesses permanent polar ice caps composed of layered water ice capped with seasonal sheets of frozen solid carbon dioxide (dry ice). During the freezing Martian winter, approximately 25% to 30% of the entire atmosphere freezes directly onto the polar caps, causing significant global atmospheric pressure variations.

Radar telemetry from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (SHARAD) confirmed that massive glaciers of relatively pure water ice lie buried beneath protective insulating blankets of rocky debris across mid-latitude valleys, providing vital water resources for future human exploration.

✦ 04. Earth-Like Sols & Seasonal Cycles

A single day on Mars—known as a Sol—lasts 24 Hours, 37 Minutes, 22 Seconds, only 39 minutes longer than an Earth day. With an axial tilt of 25.19° (nearly identical to Earth's 23.44°), Mars experiences four distinct seasons, though each season lasts almost twice as long due to its 687-day orbital year.

Because Mars has an eccentric orbit, southern summers occur near perihelion when Mars is closest to the Sun, generating intense thermal convection that regularly triggers planet-wide dust storms engulfing the entire globe for months.
